Travelling Through Space In Time#

“You’re traveling through another dimension, a dimension not only of sight and sound but of mind; a journey into a wondrous land whose boundaries are that of imagination.”

— Rod Serling, opening narration for The Twilight Zone

A.R.G.O. — Astrarium Research & Galactic Observatory

The crew stays with the observatory. The far-reaching lines are a picture of the mind-journey we want to explore; Astrarium has not calculated a physical path that carries information to the ancient sky.

Start here: what are we trying to do?#

Imagine a crew staying aboard A.R.G.O. while their minds take an excursion through a stream of distant events. The crew’s bodies remain in the cabin and their clock advances by minutes; what they see and know is connected to remote places and times by a continuous information path. Earth and the other planets keep moving, so we need their positions to understand the view and what that path passes near.

A telescope’s built-in rewind#

Zoom is time travel for the eyes. We stay on Earth while the light brings us a view from long ago. Zooming in makes a distant patch look bigger; it does not carry us there or make its light older. A telescope gathers light arriving now, and that light left its source earlier. Moonlight takes about 1.3 seconds to reach us, sunlight about 8 minutes 20 seconds, and light from the Andromeda Galaxy about 2.5 million years. We can look at Andromeda while standing still, but we see an older view of it. Astronomers call this look-back time (NASA’s Webb explainer and NASA’s Hubble explanation).

The telescope does not chase the light. It collects old light as it arrives. The longer the light travelled, the farther into the past the view reaches.

Think of a telescope as a window with a very long delivery time. The Moon sends us a picture from a little over a second ago; the Sun sends one from about eight minutes ago; a faraway galaxy sends a much older picture. We are not moving backward. The light is bringing an earlier scene to us now. In that sense, looking far away is a kind of time travel for the eyes.

Poetic physics#

Zoom in: the galaxy fills the screen.
The telescope keeps its place on Earth.
Light has crossed the dark for ages,
carrying a picture from long ago.
The farther that old light travels,
the farther back our looking reaches.
Night brings the galaxy’s yesterday
into the room where we stand tonight.

The phrase “Zoom through space and time” has three layers. A lens can enlarge the same image. A telescope can show a more distant source, whose light may be older. In our science-fiction mission, A.R.G.O. will add a mind’s-eye Zoom: a crew member chooses a remote event and experiences a continuous stream of scenes while staying at home. The first two are ordinary optics and astronomy; the third needs a new information channel. Ordinary zoom lets us observe old light already reaching us; it does not send a message to an earlier Earth.

The mind’s-eye Zoom: poetic physics with a science engine#

The telescope is our first time machine, and it has no engine. It gathers light that is already on its way. A photon from a galaxy can cross space for millions of years; when it reaches the dome, the crew reads a message from the galaxy’s younger self. Turn the telescope’s magnification ring and the picture grows, but the photon does not become older. Choose a more distant galaxy and the arriving light may have begun its journey much earlier. We zoom through space and meet the past in the light.

Now imagine the next step as science fiction. A.R.G.O. remains on Earth, its clock ticking forward. The crew chooses an event-address—place and time—in a three-dimensional star map with time as a fourth coordinate. A new field, the chronal echo, lets their shared viewpoint follow a continuous sequence of event-addresses. The bodies stay in the dome; the experience moves through a carefully specified stream of information. “Zoom” becomes a navigation verb: select a region, resolve its structure, then follow its history.

This is a journey of mind, not a claim that a person has physically crossed light-years. In the story, the field couples the local instruments to patterns associated with the selected events. The crew’s local clock can span minutes while the remote scene sequence spans years or millennia, but the mapping between those clocks is an input until the field equations derive it. A smooth slider is a schedule, not a solved route. The field must carry the scene’s information, preserve continuity, respect an energy and noise budget, and explain what a historical witness could detect.

Entanglement gives the idea a useful piece of real science. Two quantum systems can share correlations that no ordinary pair of coins can reproduce. Think of them as two copies of a score whose notes match when compared. But a score is not a courier: changing one reader’s measurements cannot send a chosen message to the other. Quantum teleportation also needs ordinary classical communication. So in A.R.G.O.’s fiction, entanglement is the matching key—a way to check that the received pattern belongs to the chosen event. The chronal echo is the invented courier. It is an additional law, not a hidden ability of entanglement.

Here is the poetic picture: light brings us a letter from the past; entanglement gives two readers matching bookmarks; the chronal echo imagines a path by which a chosen page can be read continuously. The first is astronomy. The second is established quantum correlation. The third is our science-fiction question. Its equations should tell us what is sent, how quickly it arrives, how noisy it is, what energy it costs, and how the channel avoids contradicting itself. That lets wonder and careful physics share the same dome.

Zoom in, and the old light fills the room.
The dome stays still; the stars do not come near.
We read the years folded into every photon.
Then A.R.G.O. asks a stranger question:
could a mind follow a signal through the map of events,
while the ship and its clock remain here?
The answer belongs to the field we have yet to discover—
and to the measurements that would prove it wrong.

The rest of this book gives that fictional field a demanding job description. It separates ordinary look-back from replay, event selection, and any proposed historical light. It also treats every imagined signal as something that needs a path, bandwidth, energy source, and observable prediction.

A precise rule for “zoom”#

In a nearby, nearly static patch of space, an event at distance \(D\) and emission time \(t_e\) can be seen by an observer at time \(t_o\) when a light ray connects them:

\[ c(t_o-t_e)=D,\qquad t_{\rm lookback}=t_o-t_e=\frac{D}{c}. \]

An optical zoom changes the angle or size of the image on the screen. It does not change \(D\), \(t_e\), or the photons’ travel time. A telescope’s aperture controls how much detail it can resolve: roughly, \(\theta_{\rm min}\simeq1.22\lambda/A\) for wavelength \(\lambda\) and aperture diameter \(A\) (the Rayleigh limit for an ideal circular aperture; NASA technical reference). Enlarging a blurry image makes it larger, not sharper. So the real “time travel” in a telescope is retarded seeing: ancient light reaches us now and lets us observe the source as it was when that light left.

At source distance \(D\), that angular limit corresponds to a smallest resolvable feature of approximately

\[ \ell_{\rm min}\simeq D\theta_{\rm min}\simeq\frac{1.22\lambda D}{A}. \]

Changing display magnification \(M\) changes the apparent screen angle to \(M\theta\); it does not change \(\ell_{\rm min}\) or the light-cone condition \(c(t_o-t_e)=D\). Thus the telescope’s zoom moves the picture on the screen, while choosing a more distant source moves us to older emission events and makes each resolvable patch larger.

The distance makes the detail problem severe. For an ideal one-metre aperture observing green light (\(\lambda=550\) nm), the Rayleigh angle is about \(6.7\times10^{-7}\) radians. At 1,000 light-years, that corresponds to a patch about \(6.3\times10^{12}\) metres wide—roughly 42 AU, larger than the orbit of Neptune. A 10-metre object at that distance would subtend only about \(1.1\times10^{-15}\) radians; resolving it at the same wavelength would require an ideal aperture about \(6.3\times10^{11}\) metres across, around 4 AU. Real optics, atmosphere, detector noise, and source brightness make the task harder. A star can still be detected as a point source without resolving its surface, but a telescope cannot turn that point into a detailed historical scene by magnification alone. If A.R.G.O.’s crew sees fine detail, the fictional channel must supply the equivalent information through a different physical mechanism, or the book must describe the view as unresolved.

There is also a literal, useful kind of time-zoom that needs no new physics: record light arriving at the observatory, then scrub the recording. That lets a viewer move through the camera’s past observations. It cannot show an event whose light never reached the camera, recover details the optics failed to resolve, or send anything back to the event. For a source a thousand light-years away, the photons arriving today left about a thousand years ago; an image sequence recorded over ten minutes still covers only ten minutes of arrivals.

For the fictional A.R.G.O. excursion, write the selected remote event as \(q(\tau_A)=(\mathbf{x}_q(\tau_A),t_q(\tau_A))\), where \(\tau_A\) is the crew’s ordinary, forward-running cabin clock. This is an event-selection map, not a trajectory. The ordinary telescope can supply the view only when the selected events lie on its past light cone and their photons arrive at A.R.G.O. A science-fiction channel must supply any other view. In particular, if later cabin times are mapped to earlier remote times (\(dt_q/d\tau_A<0\)), the map alone does not explain how the image’s information reaches the crew; a continuous signal path, field law, energy-momentum source, and clock relation are still required.

The interactive uses a deliberately simple schedule to make that gap visible. Let the source stay at distance \(D\), let the excursion last \(T_A\) on the cabin clock, and choose an extra history span \(T_X\). With \(f=\tau_A/T_A\) between zero and one, its selected event time is

\[ t_q(\tau_A)=t_{\rm now}-\frac{D}{c}-fT_X, \qquad \frac{dt_q}{d\tau_A}=-\frac{T_X}{T_A}. \]

At the default settings, \(D=1{,}000\) light-years, \(T_X=1{,}000\) years, and \(T_A=300\) seconds. The last frame is therefore assigned an event about 2,000 years before now, and the map’s slope is \(-200\) remote years per cabin minute. This is a precise schedule of selected events, not a speed: no signal has been integrated between those events and the cabin. The missing physics must explain how the crew receives the view while its own proper time increases.

The schedule also tells us when ordinary photons from each selected event would reach home. In this nearby, static-space example,

\[ t_{\rm arrive}=t_q+\frac{D}{c}=t_{\rm now}-fT_X. \]

At the start of the five-minute scan (\(f=0\)), the event lies on A.R.G.O.’s present past light cone and its light arrives now. Halfway through, the selected scene is 500 years earlier and its ordinary light would have reached A.R.G.O. 500 years before the scan began. At the final frame, the selected event is 2,000 years old, but its ordinary light crossed A.R.G.O.’s location 1,000 years ago. Those photons have passed, but the events are still in the crew’s causal past. In flat spacetime, a slower carrier could bring information from them to the corresponding later cabin event. For a straight, constant-speed path, the required mean speed is

\[ \beta_{\rm view}(f)=\frac{D_{\rm ly}}{D_{\rm ly}+f(T_{X,\rm yr}+T_{A,\rm yr})},\qquad \Delta\tau_{\rm carrier}=\sqrt{[D_{\rm ly}+f(T_{X,\rm yr}+T_{A,\rm yr})]^2-D_{\rm ly}^2}\;\mathrm{yr}, \]

where \(T_{A,\rm yr}\) is the cabin interval expressed in years. In the default case, the midpoint needs about \(0.667c\) and the final event about \(0.500c\); the corresponding final straight-path proper time is about 1,732 years. A massive field packet can have sublight group velocity, so this view-return leg need not violate causality if a real field is sourced by the scene and couples to A.R.G.O.’s receiver. This is a kinematic benchmark, not a derived sensor or field solution. It can return old information; it cannot let present-day A.R.G.O. choose or create the past event. That outward control—and the witness-visible light it produces—still needs a separate chronology-violating path or new causal law.

The distinction matters for the UFO premise. Seeing an old event at A.R.G.O. is a receiving process. Making a witness at that old event see A.R.G.O. is an outward influence at the witness’s location. It needs photons or another detectable interaction there, timed so they can reach the witness, and cannot follow merely from magnifying or replaying a view. The book therefore treats look-back, recorded replay, mind’s-eye event selection, and historical manifestation as four different operations.

What would the witness actually see?#

For someone to report a light in the sky, photons must reach that person’s eye. If a pattern is made at event \(E\) and the witness is distance \(d\) away, ordinary light takes \(d/c\) to arrive:

\[ t_{\rm witness}=t_E+\frac{d}{c}. \]

At 10 km that final trip takes about 33 microseconds; at 100 km it takes about 0.33 milliseconds. Those are ordinary light-travel times after the pattern exists. They do not explain how A.R.G.O.’s present-day channel could make a pattern at an earlier event. That requires the separate chronal interaction in the story.

The historical witness would not see the cabin, crew, or grounded dome just because the crew is looking through a mind’s-eye Zoom. They would see only what the channel makes happen locally: perhaps a pulse, a point of light, or a changing image. For a 7.2 m wide pattern—the approximate outside diameter in A.R.G.O.’s design—the angular width is \(\theta\simeq L/d\) when \(d\) is much larger than \(L\). It would span about 2.5 arcminutes at 10 km, or 0.25 arcminutes at 100 km. For scale, 20/20 vision resolves detail about one arcminute wide under ideal conditions (American Academy of Ophthalmology). A shape needs several resolvable details across it: if we use five ideal one-arcminute elements as a simple recognition target, the 7.2 m outline reaches that width only within about 5 km. That is an optimistic geometry limit, not a promise of recognition; darkness, atmosphere, low contrast, motion, and imperfect eyesight all make the real limit shorter. Beyond that range the same pattern could still be a bright point, but the witness would not resolve its saucer outline.

That gives the fictional drive a two-part job: carry the selected scene to the crew’s experience and, if the mission is to become a sighting, deposit or redirect a controlled light pattern at the historical event. The light-budget calculator estimates energy for a point-like flash; it does not yet calculate a resolved saucer image. A convincing mission result must predict the angular track and brightness for each witness, not merely draw a glowing disc at the target date.

In spacetime language, each dot is an event: a place and a time. A person or planet traces a worldline, a connected history through those events. Light traces a light cone, the boundary of which events can send signals to or receive signals from a chosen event under ordinary relativity. A telescope at Earth sees only events whose light has reached Earth; magnifying the image changes no event and bends no light cone. The ordinary “time travel” is in the age of the arriving photons: we see the source as it was when those photons left.

The story’s mind’s-eye Zoom adds another path, but it must not confuse three lines on the map. The hull’s worldline stays at home. The selected remote events form a continuous viewing history \(q(\tau_A)\), indexed by the crew’s forward-moving cabin time. A fictional chronal channel must connect that history to the crew’s experience. If the crew is also meant to be seen at a historical site, a separate outward signal and a local light-emission event are required; a private experience in the cabin cannot itself appear in another observer’s sky. In our present model, that appearance is an additional premise, not a consequence of looking at the old sky.

The mind’s-eye Zoom#

Now imagine the zoom control had another setting. Instead of making a picture bigger, it would choose which moment we pay attention to: a star forming long ago, a planet crossing its sun, or a bright point appearing above an ancient horizon. A.R.G.O.’s crew would remain in its cabin, yet their experience would follow a continuous stream of scenes from far away and long ago. The cabin clock might count minutes while the chosen history spans years or centuries. That is our story’s central invention: not a faster telescope, but a new connection between a nearby mind and a remote event.

We can borrow an image from quantum physics to help imagine the connection. Entangled particles can behave like two parts of one carefully prepared experiment: measure them separately and their results can be linked in a way that ordinary, independent objects cannot explain. In the story, those correlations become a kind of cosmic index—a way for A.R.G.O. to select a matching event in the sky. The index is not the journey. It does not carry a picture, a thought, or a message by itself. The fictional chronal channel must do that work, obeying its own rules for distance, time, energy, detail, and noise.

For a science-heavy version of the fiction, treat entanglement as a key, not a courier. A.R.G.O. and the destination may share a prepared quantum reference state; measurements can then help correlate or authenticate which modes belong to the same imagined channel. But the no-signalling rule still applies: the receiver’s local measurement statistics do not reveal a message chosen by the sender. The chronal field—not entanglement—must carry the changing scene. In the model, give that field an explicit state \(\Psi(x)\), an evolution/transfer law, a bandwidth and noise budget, and a stress-energy tensor. Entanglement may enter as a boundary condition or correlation resource, but any claim that it opens a past-directed communication path is a new postulate that changes ordinary causal structure and must be stated openly.

There is a sharper constraint on the “cosmic index” idea: ordinary entanglement cannot be planted in an unprepared ancient receiver, and the crew cannot choose its measurement result to encode a message. So a story in which the index points to a particular past witness must add a resource that was already shared, a global boundary condition that links the endpoints, or a new interaction at the destination. Those alternatives are different mechanisms. The first needs a credible account of how the shared state was prepared; the second changes the global causal rules; the third must carry energy, momentum, and detectable information to the witness. A.R.G.O. can use entanglement to label or verify a view inside its own laboratory, but the channel remains responsible for reaching beyond it.

So our poetic rule is simple: zoom chooses the view; the channel carries the experience. The first half is familiar: telescopes show old light, and quantum experiments reveal remarkable correlations. The second half is science fiction. We will treat the channel as a real instrument inside A.R.G.O.’s imagined world and calculate what it would need to accomplish. The equations and interactive diagrams let us ask whether the imagined voyage hangs together; they do not claim that entanglement alone can send information into the past.

The science-fiction leap: a view selected by connection#

Here is one way to imagine that mechanism without pretending that ordinary zoom already does it. In the story, A.R.G.O. carries a carefully prepared quantum system. Its parts are separated and entangled: when the crew measures one part, the results can be correlated with results from the other part, even when the parts are far apart. The crew uses that correlation as a shared label for a remote event—a little like two copies of an atlas that agree on page numbers. The label helps identify the destination in the story; it does not carry the picture there.

The key is not the broadcast. Quantum entanglement, by itself, cannot send a controllable message faster than light. The correlations only become visible when observers later compare their measurement records through an ordinary, causal channel. So the fictional A.R.G.O. needs one further ingredient: a continuous spacetime connection that lets the crew’s conscious experience follow the selected event while their bodies and cabin clock remain at home. That connection is the speculative engine of the story. It is not supplied by entanglement, dark matter, or a zoom control in a telescope.

We can still give the imagined view a data budget. A scene has a field of view, angular detail, frame rate, color depth, and noise. For an uncompressed display with \(N_x\times N_y\) pixels, \(b\) bits per pixel, and \(f\) frames per second, the raw rate is

\[ R_{\rm raw}=N_xN_ybf\quad\text{bits/s}. \]

For example, a modest \(640\times360\) monochrome view at 8 bits per pixel and 10 frames per second is \(18.4\) Mbit/s, or about \(5.5\) Gbit over five minutes before compression, error correction, or neural encoding. This is a design benchmark, not a prediction of how much information consciousness needs. The chronal channel must say what it transports—raw samples, compressed scene features, or a fictional direct experience—and give a capacity \(C\), latency, and error rate. A coherent scene requires its useful rate to exceed the chosen representation’s rate, or to state what detail is discarded. Entanglement does not erase this budget: quantum teleportation requires classical communication as well as shared entanglement, and that classical leg remains causal.

We can make the imagined device more precise by asking what it must do. It must map a chosen event (E) to a crew experience at home, preserve enough information to form a view, keep the crew’s local clock advancing by minutes, and specify how any visible light at (E) reaches a witness. Each step needs a rule; a slider that selects an ancient date is only an interface until those rules are written down. The route must also be continuous, and its path through space and time must be checked against planets, stars, radiation, and other hazards.

For the story, call this proposed link a chronal channel: a continuous connection between a present observer and a selected spacetime event. “Chronal” is a name for the fictional component, not a recognized field or particle. Entanglement can serve as the story’s correlation key or boundary condition, while a new spacetime structure or field carries the actual information. A traversable wormhole would be another speculative option, with different equations and costs. We can compare these ideas by asking what each predicts and what conservation laws, causal limits, and observations it must satisfy.

Our science-fiction design gives the channel two jobs from one shared field: it brings a changing scene into the crew’s experience, and it can excite light near a historical witness. Those are separate outputs. Entangled reference devices aboard A.R.G.O. might help compare measurements and check timing; they are not bookmarks planted in the ancient sky, and they cannot carry either output by themselves. The crew’s private view also cannot make a witness see a saucer; the model must calculate photons at the witness’s location, their direction and color, and the energy that powers them. If the field loops through its own past, the loop must settle into a self-consistent state instead of letting the crew rewrite its input however they wish. This is the chronal echo’s fictional physics contract.

There is an image-making challenge too. To make a witness see a shape instead of a dot, the light must come from a spread-out, carefully timed pattern. At 550 nm, a simple diffraction estimate says that resolving a 10 cm detail at 10 km needs an effective light-making aperture at the historical scene at least about 7 cm wide. A.R.G.O. is not carrying a projector back there, so the story’s field would have to create that extended pattern or make real material at the scene shine in just the right way. Our point-light calculator checks brightness; it does not yet create a saucer picture.

There is a quantum wrinkle: light made from empty space by the simplest photon-pair process would begin as a flickering pattern, not a steady picture. The story needs many independent light modes to smooth those fluctuations, or a seed beam whose origin and energy the channel can explain.

What is physics, and what is our fiction? The older the light, the farther back in time our telescopes see: this is measured astronomy. Entangled particles show correlations: this is measured quantum physics. No known experiment lets those correlations carry a chosen message into the past. A.R.G.O.’s mind-journey, chronal channel, and minutes-long trip across light-years are science-fiction premises. We explore them with scientific habits: state the assumptions, calculate consequences, draw the worldlines, and look for contradictions.

A small thought experiment#

Imagine two sealed boxes prepared together. Each contains a quantum token. The tokens can produce correlated answers when measured, but opening one box does not let its owner choose the answer that appears in the other. A distant friend cannot read a message from the token alone. Only after the two friends compare notes through an ordinary signal do the correlations show up. In A.R.G.O.’s imagined version, the tokens help identify a shared “channel” or viewpoint—but the story still has to invent how a picture, memory, or light signal travels along it.

This distinction makes the story more interesting, not less. The central mystery is no longer “Can we use entanglement as a magic telephone?” It becomes: What new, continuous structure of spacetime would make a mind’s viewpoint track distant events, while keeping the laboratory’s ordinary measurements consistent? That gives our equations real work to do.

Here, “dimension of mind” names the crew’s layer of experience. It does not by itself mean an extra spatial dimension in the equations.

In this book’s mission, A.R.G.O. remains at its home location while the crew experiences a mind-journey linked to an appearance associated with the Star of Bethlehem. We also ask whether a related signal effect could help explain reports of strange lights. The old story does not tell us that a ship was there, or give enough measurements to know exactly where or when the light appeared.

The hard part is that ordinary light takes as many years to cross a distance in light-years. For the crew’s minds to range across thousands of light-years while their cabin clock spans minutes, the model needs a new way to connect experience with remote events. Selecting a date on a slider does not supply that mechanism.

Astrarium can already show parts of the moving sky and draw example routes. It cannot yet calculate a working mind-journey mechanism or prove that one could exist. This book asks what a model would need to specify: how remote information reaches the crew, how local and distant clocks are related, what observers see, and how to check the information path for hazards.

A few useful words#

  • Worldline: the path of an object through space and time. In this mission, A.R.G.O.’s worldline stays near home; each signal has its own path.

  • Spacetime: a way to describe both where and when something happens.

  • Epoch: the date and time used for an event in the sky.

  • Look-back time: how long the light we see from an object has been travelling to us; the farther away the object, the older the light we receive.

  • Proper time: time measured by a clock travelling with the crew. Here it advances locally during the crew’s mind-journey through remote observations.

  • Mind-journey: the mission’s name for the crew’s experience following observations of distant events while their bodies remain aboard A.R.G.O.

  • Signal path: the continuous route taken by light or other information through a chosen spacetime model.

  • Collision or hazard check: a search for objects, fields, or conditions that could affect a modeled path.

  • Model: a set of rules and equations used to calculate an example. A model can help us learn without proving that nature or a machine works that way.

  • CTC: short for “closed timelike curve.” It is a name for a possible path in some equations that loops back to an earlier event. The name does not mean anyone has built a time machine.

  • Metric: the equations that tell us how to measure distances and times in a model of spacetime.

  • Ephemeris: a table or calculation showing where an object in space is at different times.

  • Stress-energy: a physics description of matter, energy, and pressure that can affect the shape of spacetime.

What have we learned so far?#

In flat spacetime, information carried by light takes thousands of years to cross thousands of light-years. A minutes-long mind-journey therefore needs a specified connection between the crew’s experience and the remote events. A.R.G.O. stays at home while the crew’s awareness is linked to that sequence of observations; the mathematical and physical mechanism remains an open part of the mission model.

A separate mathematical calculation follows a hypothetical traveler through a two-bubble spacetime. It does not show how to build or control the spacetime, and it does not solve A.R.G.O.’s signal mission. A second calculation estimates one kind of energy density for a simplified saucer-shaped bubble; its result is not the fuel a ship needs or a real engine design.

The book marks four kinds of statements:

  • Mission premise: the behavior we want to model.

  • Calculation: an answer that follows from stated equations and inputs.

  • Picture or simulation: a way to explore an idea on screen.

  • Unknown: something the equations, evidence, or current Astrarium code cannot yet answer.

Figures in the book are generated from calculations when the book is built. Their inputs are examples unless a source is named. A picture of a route is a way to ask a question, not proof that a ship or signal has followed it.
