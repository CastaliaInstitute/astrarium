Astrarium time travel#

The sky projector and 3D star field share one simulation clock. For modern dates its civil-time fields are UTC; for historical dates they are a UTC-like encoding of a UT1 instant because UTC did not exist. Setting a year keeps the current month, day, and clock time; the exact-instant controls use that astronomical year (including year 0 and negative BCE years). Choosing a date or stepping the shared instant by one hour, day, month, or year pauses the clock at that instant. The warp controls run the shared clock forward or backward continuously and can pause at any selected date. The projector computes the local sky from propagated star positions and selected-date solar-system calculations. The 3D scene evaluates its local Sun, Moon, and planet positions at the same epoch. The dashboard’s equatorial star map propagates Gaia-matched proper motion to the fractional year of the shared instant. Its date-equator precession matrices refresh at day boundaries, and the mobile horizon view computes horizon coordinates from that same shared instant and mean-equator frame. The native local-sky and mobile horizon calculations share Earth Rotation Angle plus the long-term precession model’s equinox shift, so local sidereal time and catalog star coordinates use the same mean equinox frame. The browser uses the native sidereal value when connected, with a conventional GMST fallback offline. Earth rotation uses the selected UTC-like instant as UT1, while compact solar-system fallback orbits use the NASA GSFC Espenak-Meeus estimate of ΔT = TT−UT to shift their dynamical epoch. The estimated ΔT and NASA’s approximate one-sigma uncertainty are exposed by the epoch API and dashboard; table values are interpolated between NASA’s published anchor years, and the dashboard also shows the corresponding uncertainty in Earth-rotation angle. Uncertainty is left unquantified for dates where NASA publishes no general estimate. Ancient ΔT values and future extrapolations are uncertain, and this model does not recover irregular Earth rotation from historical observations, so distant local-horizon orientations remain uncertain. The polynomial model is published by NASA GSFC, and its uncertainty guidance is here. The Earth Rotation Angle relation follows the IERS Conventions. Interplanetary route departure controls use the same astronomical year convention with separate month, day, and UT-like time fields, so route screening can begin at a BCE instant as well as at a modern date. “Use shared sky date” copies the current clock instant into those route fields.

For the Moon, the app requests geocentric vectors from JPL Horizons for the selected UTC day, stores the 25 hourly states in its private cache, and interpolates between them. Until that day is loaded or when the request fails, the renderer uses its compact lunar orbit approximation. The Moon source is included in the live sky and dashboard astronomy data so the fallback is visible. Earth and Moon heliocentric positions use the same lunar vector when available, correcting the Earth-Moon barycenter split used by the planet model.

The app’s route catalog contains 79 named moons of Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune, and Pluto, including the five-member Pluto system, inner ring moons, and several distant irregular satellites. Pluto’s Charon, Nix, Hydra, Kerberos, and Styx use JPL’s PLU060 solution (1800–2200); these five can also be selected as route endpoints. The local sky and 3D scene request and draw every target available in its kernel interval. The sky labels the 24 largest targets to keep the planetary fields readable; smaller moons remain visible as points. Requests are made only when the selected date lies inside each target’s kernel coverage. Jupiter’s distant Himalia is included with JPL Horizons target 506 and the JUP344 solution (1799-12-25 through 2199-01-10); Jupiter’s route encounter window includes Himalia’s roughly 11.37-million-kilometer orbit. Elara, Pasiphae, Sinope, Lysithea, Carme, Ananke, and Leda also use JUP344; their positions are tracked, while unknown radii make route clearance explicitly unknown. Those bodies are excluded from surface endpoint selection. Horizons returns these target states relative to the Sun; the sky renderers subtract Earth’s heliocentric position before converting each state into the Earth-centered line of sight used by the planets. The projector reports the number of catalogued moon kernels covering the selected instant, cached states, pending requests, and targets outside their kernel intervals. Its astronomy debug response also lists each moon’s parent, kernel, published date bounds, and state (available, loading, not_loaded, unavailable, or outside_kernel), distinguishing missing historical coverage from a state that is still loading. Route planning uses the catalogued targets near their supported parent systems. Covered states are interpolated locally; missing or out-of-kernel dates remain absent or mark route coverage incomplete. JPL lists natural-satellite trajectories as useful over decades to centuries, shorter than its planetary-system ephemerides; the 5,000-year view must therefore show kernel coverage gaps rather than implying that every moon’s position is known. See JPL’s satellite ephemeris coverage and description of trajectory limits.

Space-time navigation and collision screening#

The continuous-worldline temporal-drive candidate sets out a conditional moving-mouth wormhole reference model, the metric and clock-offset equations it assumes, and why it does not yet constitute a physically realizable drive. In particular, a negative coordinate-time rate can be a coordinate artifact; epoch must be anchored to external clocks and ephemeris event labels.

Physics boundary#

Onboard elapsed proper time (\tau), the traveler event (x^\mu(\tau)), and the displayed environment epoch (t(\tau)) are distinct. Every proposed trip must be a single connected, continuous worldline with no teleportation, epoch jump, or splice across missing trajectory segments. Changing the selected sky date in the ordinary UI is clock scrubbing; it does not model a traveler.

In ordinary globally hyperbolic general relativity, a global time function increases on future-directed causal worldlines. Thus a decreasing environment epoch while onboard time increases requires chronology-violating spacetime structure or an explicit new-physics postulate. A smooth curve for (t(\tau)) is only a kinematic prescription: it does not derive a metric, stress-energy, drive law, energy budget, stability, or causal interaction rules. Astrarium’s temporal travel premise is therefore conditional simulation physics, not an established capability. See the whitepaper’s physics boundary and references.

The planner must carry that same continuous route through every hazard check. Checking a set of sampled positions against stored object states cannot certify the unsampled intervals, and missing history remains unknown rather than safe.

Time direction is part of route playback, but the current planner does not yet model a temporal traveler. Its backward mode swaps the endpoints and epochs, solves an ordinary forward-time gravity arc, then plays its samples backward. The current dynamics do not integrate a vehicle with decreasing environment epoch while its onboard elapsed time advances. No proper-time relationship, continuous temporal-drive law, or transition path is defined. We require the actual traveler’s position and environment epoch to evolve continuously; its onboard time continues forward. Its collision checks apply only to the synthetic spatial arc against covered object histories at corresponding epochs; they do not certify the intended continuous time-travel worldline. Reverse results therefore report hypothetical_reverse_playback_incomplete, even when the synthetic arc has no modeled collision. The Apollo 11 waypoint preset uses lunar touchdown and launch as endpoints for this modeled transfer; it is not the flown Apollo trajectory. The recorded mission navigator can also play available histories backward, and its selected epoch updates the shared sky clock.

The Apollo 11 event navigator includes source-backed UTC and ground-elapsed-time waypoints for launch, translunar injection, lunar orbit insertion, spacecraft separation, landing, EVA, lunar liftoff, trans-Earth injection, and splashdown. Selecting an event sets both the shared sky instant and route date. The timeline contains event timing only; it does not imply a recovered CSM/LM position at every event. Event times are sourced from NASA’s Record of Lunar Events, Apollo 11 trajectory reconstruction report, and mission overview. The separate Apollo 11 · reverse the recorded landing segment control now plays the bundled Eagle P-66 final-descent reconstruction backward from touchdown. This is a 140-second, Moon-local downrange/altitude profile digitized from NASA TM 20220007267; it has no lateral coordinate or geodetic state. The replay stops at its source-coverage boundary. It does not bridge to launch, fabricate a CSM/LM cruise, or claim a historical collision clearance. The adjacent interplanetary planner remains an explicitly modeled route between selected body waypoints, with the available chronological hazard screens and their coverage gaps. The recorded-trajectory navigator now includes the final 140 seconds of the Apollo 11 Eagle LM’s P-66 landing phase. The local downrange/altitude profile is digitized from Figure 11 in NASA TM 20220007267, starts at GET 102:43:20, uses 0.5-second samples, and can be played forward or backward while the shared sky follows its epoch. NASA’s report reconstructs the curve from published plots because the original data were unavailable. The profile has no crossrange or geodetic track bearing; it is shown as a local profile and cannot be checked against lunar terrain. The viewer does screen the digitized centerline altitude against the report’s zero-altitude reference plane, reporting an interpolated crossing or the minimum sampled clearance. This is a limited contact screen: local relief, landing-gear/vehicle dimensions, lateral position, and reconstruction uncertainty are not modeled, so it cannot establish terrain clearance or collision safety. The profile is a real but short segment of the mission, not the CSM/LM journey from launch to return.

Archived spacecraft are screened as moving hazards using their recorded positions and the route’s interpolated positions at the union of both sample times. A gap larger than an archive’s cadence or any missing interval marks the screen incomplete. The Apollo 11 S-IVB history is a reconstructed, uncrewed upper-stage track from 1969-07-17 through 1969-07-24 at 30-minute cadence; it does not represent the crewed command/service module or lunar module. A zero modeled clearance is a detected intersection under the selected radius and margin assumptions. No detected intersection does not certify a route safe: untracked debris, catalog gaps, finite sampling, and orbit uncertainty remain outside the model.

Planet collision bounds use equatorial radii where available. Saturn’s dense D–F ring annulus is screened separately using a geometric ring-plane distance, its configured margin, and a time-varying pole orientation. A potential ring encounter is reported as a hazard rather than a solid-body collision and is also considered when ranking departure dates. The model treats the annulus as continuous; it does not resolve gaps, ring warps, or the diffuse E/G rings. Atmospheric entry, local terrain, and object/ephemeris uncertainty are separate hazards and are not included in this route screen.

Stellar positions#

The bundled bright-star catalog is cross-matched to local Gaia DR3 G < 8 astrometry. Matched coordinates, proper motions, and positive-parallax distance are stored at Gaia’s J2016.0 reference epoch. The projector propagates angular motion at constant proper-motion speed along a great circle from the catalog’s observation epoch to the selected observation year, then precesses the direction into the equator of date. For bright stars with measured Gaia DR3 radial velocity, the projector instead advances the full 3D position and velocity, including linear perspective effects. Stars without radial-velocity measurements keep the great-circle proper-motion model. Catalog positions already describe apparent directions at their observation epochs, so stellar light time is not subtracted again. This avoids tangent-plane drift over multi-millennial intervals. Constellation line vertices use the same motion data when a Gaia counterpart is available. Stars without a match keep their original J2000 coordinates and receive precession only. The radial-velocity supplement contains 53,137 Gaia DR3 sources with G < 8 and a published velocity; 476 of the current 5,044 projector catalog stars have matched radial velocities. Gaia documents its radial_velocity field in km/s in the DR3 source catalog data model. Regenerate the supplement with scripts/gaia_bright_radial_velocity.py, then rebuild the projector catalog with scripts/build_sky_catalog.py and the dashboard map with scripts/enrich_parent_star_astrometry.py.

The browser star map contains 83,468 rows, including the Sun, and currently has 81,851 Gaia J2016 astrometry entries: 81,844 matches from the local query plus seven retained matches from the earlier bright-star cross-match. The local cross-match requires positions within 0.02° and magnitudes within 2.5; it copies Gaia’s J2016.0 coordinates as well as its proper motions. The browser propagates those matches and applies the same Vondrák date-equator precession used by the projector. The remaining 1,616 unmatched map stars and named-object positions receive precession without proper motion.

The 3D Gaia tile catalog uses its packed proper motions to move stars from J2016.0 in the selected year along the same constant-speed great-circle model, preserving catalog distance. Its proper motions are quantized and can be clamped by the tile format. The bright local HYG 3D catalog also matches 447 stars to Gaia astrometry, including measured radial velocities where available. Those stars move in 3D through the selected epoch; their projected locations, named-star waypoints, and crosshair labels use the same propagated positions. Regenerate the match asset with scripts/enrich_starfield3d_astrometry.py.

The route departure-window comparison ranks transfers with no detected modeled collision and acceptable arrival targeting first, then prefers fewer known coverage gaps and greater minimum modeled clearance. It adds up to four midpoint screens in same-status coarse intervals, prioritizing intervals whose modeled clearances are nearest zero, then bisects each observed collision-status transition four times. This improves sampled coverage but can miss narrow safe or hazardous windows between samples. It identifies a preferred candidate only when those modeled checks pass; incomplete object catalogs, ephemeris gaps, and trajectory uncertainty still mean that no candidate is certified collision-safe.

Limits#

This is a visualization model, not a precision historical almanac. Gaia radial-velocity coverage is incomplete, and linear stellar motion omits Galactic acceleration, close encounters, and perspective acceleration beyond the constant velocity model. The projector uses the Vondrák, Capitaine & Wallace long-term precession model. For the selected sky date it downloads daily geometric vectors from JPL Horizons, which currently identifies DE441 as the source for these bodies, then uses cubic-Hermite interpolation. This is cached by route interval on device and fetched in the background for sky-only scrubbing. Mercury, Venus, and Earth use planet-center vectors; Mars and the outer planets, including Pluto, use DE441 system barycenters. If a vector is missing for Mercury through Neptune, the compact JPL approximate-position model is used and the view reports that fallback. Pluto has no compact fallback, so its marker is omitted until its DE441 vector is available. A Pluto moon can still appear when its own PLU060 state is available. Route status remains incomplete if its requested interval lacks Pluto’s vector. Planetary barycenter offsets remain a limitation; historical outer-planet positions carry larger uncertainty than inner-planet positions. The NASA ΔT estimate shifts only compact fallback orbital calculations; Horizons vectors are queried at the selected UT-like epoch. ΔT and historical Earth rotation remain uncertain at distant epochs.

The precession matrix implementation follows the SOFA/ERFA formulation of the Vondrák model. Its epoch 1666.666 matrix agrees with the SOFA reference test values; this numerical check does not validate the rest of the sky pipeline.

The compact fallback coefficients and stated fit range come from JPL’s approximate positions page. JPL documents DE441 as spanning 13,200 BCE to 17,191 CE, and notes that Horizons currently serves its long-range subinterval through 9999 BCE–9999 CE. Astrarium’s clock currently spans 3000 BCE–3000 CE, within that Horizons interval. The DE440/DE441 documentation recommends DE441 for historic epochs; its long-span fit has less modern-century accuracy than DE440.

The lunar state is queried from the JPL Horizons API on demand rather than bundled as a kernel. This supports the shared clock’s millennial range where Horizons responds, but is network-dependent on a cache miss and is not an offline high-precision lunar ephemeris.

The dashboard’s year field uses astronomical year numbering: year 0 is 1 BCE, and year -3000 is 3001 BCE. Artificial satellite trajectories are loaded for the selected UTC day from ETH Zurich’s public Satellite Database archive when network access is available. Results are cached by day on the device. The projector draws a one-orbit sky arc for each returned object and limits propagation to 60 days from its TLE epoch. The bundled current TLE file is used as an offline fallback. ETH Zurich began continuously collecting this archive on 2023-03-10; it has date and object gaps and is not a 1957-to-present catalog. Dates before that start or after the current day are reported outside the archive range; an empty response within the range means no records were returned for that query, not that no satellites existed. The ETH API returns dated TLE snapshots, and SGP4 propagation from a snapshot is limited here to 60 days from its epoch. It does not provide full historical state trajectories. Separately, the route planner screens 24 bundled, reconstructed interplanetary spacecraft histories from NAIF SPICE and JPL Horizons when their date coverage overlaps the route. These are mission spacecraft tracks, not the full population of Earth-orbiting satellites. They use daily or finer samples and are interpolated in ICRF/ecliptic J2000 coordinates; incomplete catalog coverage remains unknown. The interplanetary path screen samples the available catalog at 65 equally spaced times across the route. It adds TLE propagation samples for every integrated path segment whose spacecraft-to-Earth chord passes within 250,000 km: up to five minutes apart generally, refined to 30 seconds when the route passes within 50,000 km of Earth. This covers the departure and arrival regions where Earth-orbiting satellites can intersect the route. The chart draws up to 2,000 historical satellite tracks that pass within 250,000 km of the vehicle, sampled from those route epochs; it breaks tracks at archive gaps longer than 15 minutes rather than connecting across missing data. It reports how many time samples had usable records, how many are pending, failed, stale, empty, or outside the archive date range, and the closest sampled clearances for returned NORAD objects. It also propagates those records into the heliocentric route plot. Per-object closest-approach rows include the age and provenance of the selected orbital element set at that time (ETH archive, user import, or bundled fallback). This is not a continuous satellite catalog: route planning fetches sampled UTC days concurrently within an eight-second archive wait budget, and uses only snapshots cached by the end of that prefetch plus the bundled fallback. Therefore missing samples, uncataloged objects, and route periods outside an element set’s 60-day validity remain unknown. Between route samples up to five minutes apart (30 seconds in the innermost screening zone), closest approach uses cubic Hermite interpolation from the integrated vehicle state and SGP4 satellite position and velocity, with a numerical minimum search whenever the straight chord estimate falls within the safety margin plus 10,000 km. More distant intervals keep the chord estimate. This refines likely encounters without running the full minimizer for every satellite and interval. It represents orbital curvature better than a straight chord near a potential encounter, but element changes, model and frame uncertainty, catalog gaps, and intervals longer than five minutes remain unknown. The screen uses a 1 km assumed satellite collision radius plus the user margin and is not a conjunction certification.

Both visual arcs and route checks use the Vallado SGP4/SDP4 implementation with WGS-72 constants to propagate archived TLEs into TEME positions. The model includes the standard near-Earth and deep-space perturbation terms, but TLE age, catalog omissions, the route’s finite sampling, and the simplified path model still make the result unsuitable for conjunction or collision certification. The archive contains public catalog TLE records, but object/time coverage varies and cannot provide a complete trajectory for every satellite. Astrarium labels the archive record count and reports when the selected date has no returned records. The shared sky clock spans 3000 BCE–3000 CE; artificial satellite records begin with the space age and have gaps.

The historical records are fetched from the ETH Zurich Satellite Database API, which provides archived TLEs with selectable time ranges. The database states that continuous collection began on 2023-03-10 (about the archive). A TLE is an orbital-element snapshot, not a continuous archived state history. The short track drawn by Astrarium is propagated from the nearest returned element set.

Older user-obtained two-line TLE, three-line TLE/3LE, SGP4 OMM JSON, or GP CSV files can be imported from the dashboard’s Historical satellite elements control. OMM JSON supports full catalog identifiers that do not fit in the TLE five-character field. Astrarium groups them by each element set’s own epoch, keeps the source snapshots on device, and selects the closest per-object element within the SGP4 60-day limit for the selected sky date and each route sample. Re-importing overlapping TLE files deduplicates exact element lines; OMM rows deduplicate by catalog ID and normalized UTC epoch. Changed day files are written through a same-directory temporary file and atomically replaced. These records appear in the local sky when visible and are included in route screening; an imported-only date remains marked partial. The dashboard also summarizes the persisted imported snapshot count, object count, UTC epoch-day count, and first-to-last epoch range after reload, with buttons to jump the shared sky clock to the first or latest snapshot and a selector for each stored UTC snapshot date. Its NORAD object browser lists the imported epochs for one catalog ID and can jump the shared sky clock to any of those element records. The plot shows archived element-epoch positions as separate points, plus the selected epoch’s nearest SGP4-propagated one-orbit arc. It does not connect observations from different dates or claim a continuous reconstructed history between element sets. Positions use a TEME-to-ecliptic-J2000 approximation with long-term precession; nutation and equation-of-equinoxes corrections are omitted. The archive-wide range does not imply continuous coverage. For ordinary sky and route queries, imported files are scanned only within 60 days of each requested epoch; the complete imported archive is not retained as live SGP4 objects in memory. Summary scans read only record IDs and epochs, and per-object browsing instantiates only that object’s element sets. Imported records augment the ETH archive and do not imply complete catalog coverage. TLE and OMM records both remain element-set snapshots, and propagation stays limited to 60 days from their epoch. Space-Track GP_History and CelesTrak archived OMM exports can supply additional historical records, but Astrarium does not fetch them directly or claim a complete catalog. CelesTrak offers unclassified historical GP records from 1957 through its latest update, but its special-request workflow limits each request to 100 objects and 20,000 records, delivers results by email, and requires a CAPTCHA; its TLE format is limited to five-digit catalog numbers. Astrarium accepts TLE/3LE text, SGP4 OMM JSON, and GP CSV with the required SGP4 element columns. See the CelesTrak historical archive request.

Space-Track documents more than 138 million historical general-perturbation element sets in GP_History. It requires an account and is subject to its query guidelines; large or long-range queries should use its yearly archive bundles. For a specific period, export GP_History in JSON/OMM format and import the files through the dashboard’s Historical satellite elements control, which accepts multiple date-sliced files in one selection (each file up to 20 MB) or a ZIP bundle up to 100 MB. ZIP import reads TLE, 3LE, OMM JSON, and GP CSV entries without extracting archive paths, caps each expanded entry at 20 MB and the whole archive at 200 MB, and validates the complete ZIP before importing any contained records. Invalid data entries are reported individually; valid entries from the same archive can still be imported. The importer accepts Space-Track’s UTC EPOCH values with or without a trailing timezone suffix, groups records by element epoch, and the route viewer propagates the closest valid element set for each object. This can extend a user’s local history back to early satellite records, but it cannot reconstruct periods or objects absent from the downloaded archive. See the official Space-Track API documentation.

For routes passing near Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune, or Pluto, the planner requests JPL Horizons vectors for 79 named moons. Targets use their own published kernel intervals: for example, the Saturnian inner-satellite solution SAT415 covers 1990–2018, while Uranian inner-satellite solution URA184 covers 1900–2100. Pluto’s five major moons use PLU060, with coverage from 1800–2200. The route collision screen represents Pluto with its DE441 system barycenter and uses a 3,350 km conservative envelope around that point, covering Pluto’s radius plus the Charon-driven barycenter offset. The inputs come from JPL’s Pluto satellite mean elements and physical parameters. Neptune’s listed irregular moons expand the encounter screen to include Nereid’s distant orbit. Returned states are cached on device and shown on the route plot. JPL’s current catalog lists many more objects than this curated set; the route time window is also capped. Outside a target’s kernel interval, for unlisted moons, or when a request or sample window is incomplete, coverage is unknown. This is not a complete natural-satellite catalog. JPL publishes the live kernel spans in its planetary-satellite ephemeris table and Pluto’s elements in the mean-elements table.

The route origin and destination selectors also include the catalogued moons. For those endpoints the planner obtains heliocentric Horizons vectors at the departure and arrival instants and uses each moon’s physical radius when placing the transfer endpoints. The clearance point is placed along the local radial direction away from the moon’s parent. The transfer still assumes an instantaneous departure or capture there; it does not integrate a launch or landing through the local moon’s gravity. Kernel gaps are reported as unknown rather than replaced with an invented orbit. The transfer dynamics still use Sun and major-planet point-mass gravity and do not model the selected moon’s local escape trajectory or powered capture; moon-to-moon routes therefore remain coarse path screens. The planner marks these routes incomplete_coverage because selected-moon gravity and powered departure/capture are not integrated.

The initial route uses the short-way zero-revolution Lambert branch. If a preliminary route detects a planet or listed-moon collision, or clearance within 100,000 km of the configured safety boundary, Astrarium also evaluates the long-way Lambert branch and chooses the branch with fewer preliminary modeled collisions, then greater minimum planet/moon clearance. It endpoint-corrects that branch and runs the full collision screen afterward. This branch choice is shown with the preliminary collision counts and clearances used in the decision. It does not optimize against asteroids, Earth satellites, or recorded spacecraft; those still require a separate departure-window search. Neither branch is a certified flight plan.

Route integration includes the Sun, major planets, and Earth’s Moon. For close approaches to other moon systems, the planner first samples a preliminary route, fetches covered Horizons moon states near those encounters, and re-shoots the transfer with interpolated point-mass forces for moons with populated JPL gravitational parameters. The current GM table covers selected major moons; other catalogued moons are still checked for collision but do not bend the integrated route. For the DE441 system-barycenter vectors, covered moon offsets also shift the modeled primary center; Pluto’s combined system GM is reduced by the represented satellite GMs to avoid adding those masses twice. Missing moon states or GM values are reported as incomplete gravity coverage. Moon trajectories and gravity therefore remain too sparse and incomplete for collision certification.

Reported aerial observations#

The projector can display a reported aerial observation as an event in local sky coordinates. The bundled sky/ufo-sightings.json pair is a synthetic geometry fixture, not witness data or a NUFORC export. Each record carries a UTC observation time, observer latitude/longitude, reported azimuth/elevation, angular uncertainty, source attribution, and optional range and event-match fields. The dashboard’s UTC sky-instant control and observation picker set the exact replay time and pause the shared sky clock there; moving the warp control off zero resumes it. The renderer shows an event only within its stated timestamp uncertainty (at the exact second when that uncertainty is absent). A single sightline requires the configured observer to be within roughly 17 km of the report location; a triangulated estimate can be viewed from other configured sites. Reported events are not treated as recurring objects.

Azimuth/elevation define an observer’s line of sight. They do not determine range, so a report without measured range is not assigned a 3D craft position. If the source supplies a range, Astrarium combines it with the bearing and shows the result as a range-based estimate with its range uncertainty when provided. When two or more observations share an eventId, have reported directions and angular/time uncertainty values, their time-uncertainty intervals overlap, the observations span no more than ten minutes, and their sight lines have adequate crossing angle, Astrarium computes a least-squares closest point in a WGS‑84 Earth-fixed frame. It rejects behind-observer, underground, very high-altitude, and high-residual solutions; triangulation requires an explicit angular uncertainty for every bearing. The estimated position and RMS miss distance are derived values, not reported facts; the current display uses the estimate to reproject the marker for the selected observer. Optional rangeKm and rangeUncertaintyKm fields are reserved for sourced estimates. Ingested public reports must preserve their original identifiers, source URLs, precision, and reuse terms; do not infer sky direction from a city-level location or narrative alone. Import redistributable source data with the documented CSV importer. NUFORC’s terms of service claim ownership or control over its sighting database compilation, so Astrarium must not copy its records into the bundled catalog without permission; use a licensed feed or records whose rights permit redistribution. NASA’s UAP study also notes that missing metadata and calibrated corroborating measurements limit reliable conclusions from many witness reports.
