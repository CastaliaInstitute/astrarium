Trajectory planning through time and space#

Product goal#

Let a user define a departure state, destination, and time window, then explore candidate paths through a moving celestial environment. A path is a worldline: spacecraft position and velocity are evaluated at each simulation time, against body positions at that same time. The display should show when the spacecraft passes bodies and its closest approach to each one.

“Collision-free” should mean that the entire path clears each modeled body’s physical radius plus a configurable safety margin. Checking only sampled waypoints is insufficient because a fast path can cross a body between samples.

Model layers#

Solar-system navigation#

  • Treat onboard elapsed time τ and environment epoch t(τ) as separate state variables. A forward-experiencing traveler moving into the past has dt/dτ < 0 in the reduced display model; this is not reverse playback of an ordinary orbit and is not, by itself, a GR solution. Ordinary future-directed causal motion in a globally hyperbolic spacetime cannot run backward along a global time function. Treat past-directed epoch evolution as a conditional postulate requiring an explicit causal spacetime or new-physics model. Require one continuous worldline and continuous epoch evolution with no jumps, event splices, or instantaneous reassignment. Until compatible dynamics and uncertainty are modeled, return a hypothetical, incomplete result and do not claim the temporal route itself is collision-screened.

  • Use a single dynamical time scale internally (TDB) and convert to calendar dates only for the interface.

  • Obtain time-dependent Sun, planet, and Moon states from a JPL planetary ephemeris. DE431/DE441 cover the multi-millennial interval relevant to Astrarium; JPL distributes them as SPICE kernels. The compact JPL approximate element model already integrated into Astrarium is useful for visualization, but its stated outer-planet errors are too large to claim safe passage.

  • Represent a vehicle by position, velocity, and optional thrust/control history. Integrate its path under selected gravity sources; expose the model assumptions and accuracy with the result.

  • Search candidate departure times and control parameters, reject paths that hit a body’s radius plus safety margin, then rank the survivors by travel time, fuel, or clearance.

  • Find closest approach continuously along integrated arcs. Use adaptive stepping and root/minimum searches so collision checks cannot tunnel through a body between coarse samples.

Satellites and coverage#

“All satellites” includes two different populations, and neither has complete trajectory data over the full 5,000-year sky window:

  • Natural satellites: use the JPL/NAIF SPK ephemeris for each moon where its time coverage includes the route. JPL’s published files cover selected moons, with different start and stop dates; many listed windows are only a few centuries. The planetary DE431/DE441 kernel does not by itself provide every moon’s detailed orbit.

  • Artificial satellites and spacecraft: use archived state histories or mission SPKs for the specific object and interval. The currently integrated ETH Zurich TLE archive begins continuous collection on 2023-03-10 and has gaps; it cannot provide trajectories for all satellites since 1957. Earth-orbiting objects need historical tracking data such as archived GP/TLE sets, and those records have varying accuracy. Spacecraft trajectories generally need mission-specific archives. An object has no trajectory before launch, and a present-day orbit cannot safely be propagated back through unknown maneuvers or drag history.

  • Astrarium bundles 25 reconstructed Sun-centered mission histories from NAIF SPICE and JPL Horizons, including planetary probes, orbiters, and landers. Route screening now reads records whose catalog coverage overlaps the trip, converts their ICRF vectors to ecliptic J2000, and estimates closest approach by interpolating between adjacent samples. This improves the sampled-object screen, but the catalog is not a census of spacecraft and interpolation does not certify collision avoidance.

  • Store each object’s identity, physical size where known, source, reference frame, time scale, coverage interval, and position uncertainty alongside its trajectory. Route checks must treat an uncovered interval as unknown, never as empty space.

  • Screen major planets against bounding spheres using the JPL equatorial radius where available, so the reference ellipsoid is not reduced to a mean-radius sphere. This is a surface-intersection screen only: atmospheric entry, local terrain, and body/ephemeris uncertainty require separate hazard models.

  • Saturn’s dense D–F ring region has a separate plane-and-radius encounter screen. It treats the region from 66,900 to 141,000 km from Saturn’s center as a continuous annulus with a 1 km half-thickness and applies the route’s configured margin. Pole orientation uses NASA’s linear-in-century Saturn pole model transformed into the route frame. A flagged result means the geometric zone enters the margin; it does not predict particle impacts. Ring gaps, warps, the diffuse E/G rings, and uncertainties remain unmodeled.

  • Report “no modeled collision found within covered data and stated margins,” rather than claiming a route is safe when relevant object coverage or uncertainty is missing.

Interstellar and galactic context#

  • Keep the Gaia proper-motion and radial-velocity model for moving stars.

  • Treat the Milky Way potential as a large-scale context model, not as a source of precise local navigation forces.

  • Make solar-system and interstellar navigation separate scales in the integrator, since their distances, times, and useful force models differ by many orders of magnitude.

User interaction#

  1. Choose a start location, destination, departure date, and allowed travel interval.

  2. Generate one or more candidate paths.

  3. Scrub simulation time to move both the vehicle and celestial bodies.

  4. Inspect closest approach, clearance margin, and the assumptions used for each candidate.

  5. Reject a path if any modeled body’s uncertainty or the trajectory’s numerical error consumes its clearance margin.

The visual timeline should make time the shared control for the sky, body positions, vehicle position, and route marker. The current year selector can seed the departure date, but a route needs finer time resolution than a year. The dashboard route scrubber now follows changes to the shared sky clock made through the instant controls or warp, clamping its plotted vehicle marker to the nearest route endpoint and explicitly labeling sky instants outside the route interval. Scrubbing the route still writes the selected instant back to the shared clock. Vehicle, planet, natural-moon, and small-body markers are interpolated by their recorded UTC epochs; moon and small-body markers disappear outside their available track intervals. Archived satellite markers appear only inside sampled track segments, which are collected near the vehicle path and do not represent a complete orbit or a complete satellite census.

Current implementation boundary#

Astrarium currently has historical sky rendering, on-demand JPL Horizons DE441 planetary vectors with a compact approximate-position fallback, a JPL Horizons lunar vector cache with a simplified orbit fallback, simplified local orbit demonstrations, a fixed smooth Milky Way potential for stellar context, and playback of recorded terrain trajectories. The dashboard solves a zero-revolution Lambert transfer between points just above selected endpoint bodies, uses that Lambert result as an initial guess, then corrects departure velocity with finite-difference single shooting under the modeled gravity field. The vehicle is integrated under Sun, planet, and Earth-Moon point-mass gravity with adaptive-step RK4, and checks closest approach against the Sun, planets, and Earth’s Moon over each integration segment. Before a full route, Astrarium downloads one-day heliocentric vectors for Mercury, Venus, Earth, and the Mars through Neptune system barycenters from JPL Horizons (DE441), caches them by route interval, and uses cubic-Hermite interpolation at RK4 stages and during close-approach refinement. If any body fetch is unavailable, that body uses the compact approximate model and planetary coverage is marked incomplete. The model’s Mars and outer-planet system barycenters omit their moon-induced offsets from planet centers. For a Moon departure or arrival, the route planner prepares the selected endpoint day’s lunar Horizons vector before integration; it reports missing endpoint coverage and uses the compact lunar fallback when unavailable. The route model uses lunar vectors already cached for other sampled dates and falls back to the compact model outside cached dates. The separate natural-moon encounter screen uses JPL Horizons vectors where their coverage permits. RK4 step doubling controls the integration step and reports the estimated maximum local position error, accepted and rejected steps, and arrival miss distance. Endpoint correction is bounded to three iterations and reports the starting Lambert miss and final miss; its 1,000 km convergence tolerance does not include body-ephemeris uncertainty. The local integration estimate is not a global trajectory error bound. Every accepted integration segment is screened with a relative-motion arc bound, then eight-way bracketing and cubic Hermite local minimum searches refine possible close passes. This reduces missed encounters between accepted steps but is not a certified event detector or a formal closest-approach bound. The model omits powered departure and arrival maneuvers, uncertainty in the compact JPL body positions, and several object populations. Gravity is softened inside modeled physical radii so an intersecting path can be integrated far enough to report a clearance flag; this is a numerical convenience and not a physical model of passage through a body. For close passes of Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune, the route screen requests time-tagged states for 66 named moons whose Horizons targets use the configured JPL SPK families, and caches each response on device. The catalog includes ring moons, Neptune’s distant irregular moons, and previously covered major moons. The screening regions include Phoebe’s distant Saturn orbit and Nereid’s distant Neptune orbit. Targets with separate SPK solutions report their own kernel family and date interval, including the shorter SAT415 and URA184 ranges. These calls are limited to an hourly grid around a close parent-system approach and refined between samples; they are not continuous event detection. The encounter window is capped, and a capped or uncovered window is reported as incomplete. Many irregular and unlisted moons, most asteroids and comets, debris, and some spacecraft remain uncovered; Earth-orbiting satellite records are sparse and SGP4-propagated from archived TLEs. A “no modeled collision” result remains incomplete and is not a flight-safety claim. Route moon coverage can only be evaluated inside the published per-system SPK date ranges. Small inner-moon radii are approximate literature values used as collision spheres; uncertainty in shape, ephemeris, and the trajectory remains outside the reported clearance.

For a route, Horizons also supplies daily vectors for eight curated small bodies: Ceres, Pallas, Vesta, Hygiea, Interamnia, Eros, Apophis, and Itokawa. Their vector tracks and minimum modeled clearances are shown on the route plot. The collision spheres use nominal radii; radius and orbit uncertainty are not included in the clearance. This selection is not a census of asteroids or comets, and the planner keeps overall coverage incomplete even when every selected target is available.

The dashboard can compare 2–9 evenly spaced departure candidates over a user-selected window (up to ±3,650 days). It ranks candidates without detected planetary/Earth-Moon collisions before candidates with a detected collision, then by minimum clearance from those modeled bodies. Transfer candidates whose integrated path misses the destination are labeled transfer_miss; other non-collision candidates remain incomplete because natural moons and satellites are not evaluated during this lightweight comparison. This is a sparse search, not an optimized launch-window solution; satellite archive coverage is not evaluated during this lightweight comparison. Selecting a candidate runs the full route screen, which requests the exact UTC dates used for its 65 baseline satellite samples and additional five-minute epochs near Earth in parallel under a bounded wait. It checks closest relative chord approach between consecutive satellite and vehicle samples when both records are present and no more than five minutes apart, using a 1 km assumed satellite collision radius plus the user margin. Missing samples and longer intervals remain unknown. Minimum-clearance reporting includes the sampled satellite catalog when that screen is enabled; the check does not certify conjunction avoidance. The sample count includes any fresh usable TLE states, including user-imported and bundled snapshots; interval coverage remains incomplete unless the online archive reports data at every adjacent sampled interval.

Recorded probe flights#

The projector also replays spacecraft histories on a Sun-centered J2000/ICRF map. The Phoenix cruise and Mars EDL products come from the NAIF mission SPICE archive. Additional mission histories are ingested from JPL Horizons using each spacecraft’s Horizons target ID and Sun-centered geometric vectors. The imported records retain the requested TDB coverage, target ID, service response metadata, and one-day sample cadence. Horizons products can contain tracking reconstructions or propagated extensions; their source notes and coverage stay attached to the replay. Daily samples smooth over short encounters and maneuvers. A surface-relative EDL or orbital pass is a separate body-fixed record, as shown by Phoenix EDL and the MRO Jezero pass.

To add a cruise history, use scripts/ingest_horizons_trajectory.py with the probe’s Horizons ID and a date range inside its published ephemeris coverage. The projector discovers installed records through player/app/src/main/assets/trajectories/catalog.json.

The current catalog is a curated initial set, not a claim that every spacecraft ever launched has coverage. Horizons availability, target ephemeris intervals, archive reconstruction, and useful sampling cadence vary by mission. Each inertial catalog entry declares its coordinate origin; Phoenix SPICE is Sun centered, and JPL Horizons entries are Sun centered. Expand the catalog from the NAIF/PDS SPICE archive and other mission archives while recording those limits for each entry.

The installed catalog currently contains the Apollo 11 S-IVB post-separation heliocentric departure reconstruction (1969-07-17 to 1969-07-24), an Apollo 11 LM P-66 final-descent local profile (140 seconds at 0.5-second cadence), Phoenix cruise and EDL, the MRO Jezero pass, and 21 Sun-centered JPL Horizons mission histories: Voyager 1 and 2, Cassini, Galileo, New Horizons, Juno, Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter, Mars Odyssey, Mars Express, MAVEN, Curiosity, Perseverance, Dawn, Rosetta, MESSENGER, Parker Solar Probe, OSIRIS-REx, BepiColombo, NEAR Shoemaker, InSight, and Tianwen-1. The Horizons histories use one-day samples from the target-specific coverage dates recorded in the catalog; the two Phoenix SPICE tracks and MRO local pass retain their finer source cadences.

Regenerate the Apollo 11 LM P-66 local-profile asset with scripts/digitize_apollo11_lm_descent.py. The script downloads NASA TM 20220007267, renders Figure 11, and records the chart-derived downrange and altitude with source interval and limitations. It requires Poppler pdftoppm and Pillow. NASA reports the source raw data were no longer available, so this is a digitization of the report’s reconstruction, not original telemetry.

Remaining trajectory work#

Reverse-time route traversal#

The route screen accepts a time direction. Backward mode swaps the endpoints and epochs, solves an ordinary forward-time Lambert/gravity arc from the earlier destination to the later origin, then presents the samples in reverse order. This does not model a forward-experiencing traveler moving toward a decreasing environment epoch. The arc is screened against available object states at matching chronological epochs, but this geometry cannot establish clearance for a physical temporal traveler or a time-transition mechanism. The dashboard includes an Apollo 11 waypoint preset from lunar touchdown back to launch time; that preset is a modeled Earth–Moon transfer, not the flown CSM/LM states. The separate Apollo 11 recorded replay reverses the source-backed 140-second Eagle P-66 local profile and stops at its data boundary; it does not bridge that segment to launch. A path may be scrubbed backward. This mode is reverse playback of an ordinary forward-time gravity solution with endpoints swapped. It is a visualization convention, not a solution to continuous reverse-epoch dynamics or a model of an actual temporal traveler. It defines neither the traveler’s proper time nor a continuous temporal-drive law. The collision screen evaluates the synthetic spatial arc against objects at those epochs, but cannot certify a time-travel worldline. Reverse results use the explicit status hypothetical_reverse_playback_incomplete, even when no collision is detected on the sampled modeled arc. Ordinary archive/kernel coverage limits still apply.

Sun-centered recorded trajectory playback accepts forward or backward time direction. It maps the selected sample’s ET/TDB epoch to the shared sky clock, so the observer sky and solar-system ephemerides follow the recorded route in either direction. The Apollo 11 LM P-66 profile also plays in both directions and updates the shared sky clock. NASA’s 2022 reconstruction provides the final 140 seconds as downrange and altitude digitized from a published plot; it is a local profile, not a geodetic state vector. Its lateral orientation is absent, so lunar terrain clearance is not established. The player checks the digitized centerline altitude against the zero-altitude reference plane, but does not model terrain relief, vehicle dimensions, lateral position, or reconstruction error. Playback stops at the final sample. The bundled Apollo 11 S-IVB track is a NASA-contracted Earth-departure reconstruction served by JPL Horizons (target -399110), covering 1969-07-17 17:00 to 1969-07-24 00:00 UTC at 30-minute cadence. It is the discarded S-IVB stage, not the crewed command or lunar module; it must not stand in for Apollo 11’s full flown trajectory.

  • Replace the compact JPL approximation with a selectable SPICE ephemeris kernel for intervals where its coverage and accuracy are appropriate.

  • Expand natural-satellite targets beyond the current 66 named moons and replace hourly close-approach sampling with coverage-aware continuous event detection and uncertainty bounds.

  • Add planetary perturbations and an explicit departure/arrival maneuver model.

  • Replace chord-based close-approach estimates with adaptive event finding and numerical error bounds.

  • Expand natural-moon, asteroid, debris, and spacecraft coverage with explicit source and interval manifests.

  • Search launch windows and transfer parameters, then rank converged candidates by time, fuel, and clearance.
